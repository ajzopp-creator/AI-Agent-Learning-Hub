# Intro to Options

## Core ideas

- Short-term prices are often influenced by trader sentiment, which shows up in implied volatility (IV).
- Trader sentiment can be irrational, and novice traders often follow the herd while professional traders try to lead price moves.
- Long stock traders buy shares first and profit if the stock price rises.
- Short stock traders sell borrowed shares first and profit if the stock price falls.
- Short selling requires a margin account.

## Long and short stock examples

- A long stock example: buying 100 shares at $50 and selling at $55 produces a $500 profit.
- A short stock example: shorting 100 shares at $50 and buying them back at $45 produces a $500 profit.
- In both cases, the gain or loss is driven by the price change multiplied by the share count.

## Price movement basics

- Traders study charts for signals such as trends, chart patterns, candlestick shapes, breakouts, and moving-average crossovers.
- Candlesticks display the open, high, low, and close for a selected time interval.
- Reviewing multiple timeframes, such as weekly, daily, hourly, and 5-minute charts, helps traders understand broader context.
- Counter-trend trades are generally avoided unless support or resistance is being tested and the setup is backed by historical price behavior.

## Breakouts

- Price breakouts often begin with volatility compression followed by volatility expansion.
- Heavy buying or selling can trigger directional breakouts, either upward or downward.
- Professional and institutional traders may exploit breakouts early.
- Large institutional orders can move price sharply and sometimes create whipsaw action when positions are reversed for profit-taking.

## Moving averages

- Many traders use moving averages such as the 9-, 15-, 20-, 50-, and 200-period simple moving averages (SMAs).
- An SMA reflects the average price over a chosen number of periods tied to the chart timeframe.
- Exponential moving averages place more weight on recent prices.
- The moving-average length should match the intended trade duration and chart interval.

## Crossovers

- A price crossover occurs when price moves above or below a moving average.
- Another crossover occurs when one indicator line crosses another.
- Because price often oscillates around its moving average, traders watch for both breakout continuation and mean reversion.
- Shorter-term moving averages tend to produce faster but noisier signals.

## Bollinger Bands and volatility

- Bollinger Bands track volatility around a centerline that is commonly an SMA(20).
- The outer bands are typically set two standard deviations above and below the centerline.
- When the bands contract, traders often anticipate a breakout.
- When the bands expand sharply, traders may expect volatility to later contract toward more normal levels.

## Implied volatility

- Implied volatility reflects current market sentiment as expressed through option prices and trading activity.
- IV often rises during bearish conditions because traders expect prices to fall faster than they rise.
- Many experienced traders look for underlyings with elevated IV because option premiums may be richer than normal.
- Since IV often reverts toward historical volatility, traders may position for both volatility contraction and time decay.

## Option-related trade ideas mentioned

- If a stock may rally and implied volatility is increasing, one idea is to buy the stock and sell an out-of-the-money call as a covered call.
- Another idea mentioned is combining a long call with a short put to help finance the call, though that structure adds significant directional risk.
- Monitoring the option chain after a volatility signal can help identify potential strategies.

## Practical takeaway

- Study price trends across multiple timeframes before entering a trade.
- Match indicators to the expected holding period.
- Treat implied volatility as both a sentiment signal and an options-pricing input.
- Remember that high IV and unusual volume can create opportunity, but they can also increase risk.
